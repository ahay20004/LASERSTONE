WORKED PHYSICS EXAMPLE · SYMBOLIC

Light attenuation in a uniform absorbing medium

1 · Observe the phenomenon

Compare incident and transmitted intensity qualitatively; do not infer a coefficient without measurements.

Observation: transmitted intensity can decrease with propagation distance in an absorbing medium.

I(z) = I₀ exp(−αz)

I₀: incident intensity at z = 0; I(z): intensity at depth z; α: attenuation coefficient (inverse length).

Model conditions: a homogeneous medium, constant α, and a single exponential attenuation law; reflection, scattering and complex geometry require additional treatment.

Validation path: measure transmitted intensity at known depths, fit the attenuation law, report uncertainty, and check systematic deviations. No measured data are supplied here.

Teaching example only · A symbolic equation is not evidence of experimental agreement or numerical validation.

SCIENTIFIC EXPLORER · LESSON 01

Spontaneous and stimulated emission

Explore how excited atoms emit light, why stimulated emission enables optical amplification, and how photon energy depends on wavelength.

Spontaneous emission

An excited system emits without an incident stimulating photon. For an isolated radiative channel, the rate is proportional to the excited population.

Rsp = A21N2

A₂₁: spontaneous transition rate per excited system (s⁻¹).

Stimulated emission

A resonant radiation field can induce an excited system to add a photon to the stimulating optical mode.

Rst = B21ρ(ν)N2

ρ(ν): spectral radiation energy density (J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹).

Photon-energy calculator

Vacuum relation: E = hν = hc/λ. This calculates photon energy, not laser gain or emission probability.

Photon energy1.959 eV
Frequency473.76 THz
Energy in joules3.139 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

Assumptions: a photon in vacuum; wavelength is the vacuum wavelength; energy levels are not independently identified.

Condition for net stimulated gain

B21N2 > B12N1

In a two-level transition model, stimulated emission must exceed absorption. Cavity losses must also be overcome for laser oscillation.

Scientific status: established textbook relations and a deterministic educational calculation; no measured populations, validated gain, or live physics engine.

References: Einstein (1917); Siegman, Lasers (1986); Svelto, Principles of Lasers (2010). Svelto textbook DOI
